Dark beaches, safe hatchlings: what loggerhead turtles need from us

17 November 2026 · 4 min read

“My family swam with the dinosaurs,” Carla likes to say. Loggerhead turtles have outlasted a great deal. What trips them up today is surprisingly ordinary: a bright lamp and a floating bag.

Carla is a loggerhead sea turtle (Caretta caretta), the most common sea turtle in the Mediterranean. Each summer, females haul themselves onto sandy beaches at night, dig a nest and lay their eggs. About two months later the hatchlings dig out, usually after dark, and race for the water.

The Mediterranean's turtle beaches

A recent review put the average at about 8,179 loggerhead nests a year across the whole Mediterranean, with roughly 2,822 of them on the coast of Türkiye. Greece, Cyprus and Libya hold most of the rest. In Antalya alone, a monitoring programme reported that nearly 25,000 loggerhead hatchlings reached the sea during the 2026 season.

Those numbers sound large, but very few hatchlings survive to adulthood. Every one that makes it to the water counts.

Why the dark matters

On a natural beach, the brightest direction at night is out over the sea. Hatchlings crawl toward it. Hotels, beach bars, street lamps and phone torches pull them the wrong way. Researchers have shown that a long crawl toward a landward light can leave hatchlings less able to find the sea at all, even after the light is gone.

The problem doesn't stop at the waterline. When scientists tracked green turtle hatchlings off Western Australia, 90 per cent of them swam toward artificial light in the water instead of heading offshore.

Jellyfish or plastic bag?

Adult loggerheads eat jellyfish among many other things. Sea turtles hunt mostly by sight, and a study that modelled what a turtle actually sees found they prefer soft, see-through debris that looks like jellyfish. To a hungry turtle, a drifting bag can look exactly like lunch.

Two habits that help

  • Keep nesting beaches dark at night. In nesting season (roughly May to September), skip torches and phone lights on the sand, don't build fires, and ask hotels and beach bars to shield or switch off beach-facing lights.
  • Take your trash home, especially plastic bags. Anything that blows off a beach can end up drifting like a jellyfish.

If you see a nest marker or a hatchling on the sand, give it space and let the local monitoring team know. Don't carry hatchlings to the water: the crawl helps them get ready for the swim.
