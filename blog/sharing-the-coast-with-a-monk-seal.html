Fewer than 1,000 left: how to share the coast with a monk seal

17 November 2026 · 4 min read

“You've probably never seen me,” says Mo. That's true for most people, and it's part of the problem: it is hard to protect an animal nobody notices.

Mo is a Mediterranean monk seal (Monachus monachus). The most recent estimate puts the whole world population at only 815 to 997 animals. They live in three places: the eastern Mediterranean, the Cap Blanc peninsula in Mauritania and the Madeira archipelago.

A seal that hides in caves

Monk seals rest and give birth in sea caves, often on remote stretches of coast. Many of these caves have a small sandy beach inside. For a mother and her pup, the cave is the one place they can rest out of reach.

That is why disturbance matters so much. Boats that crowd a cave entrance, swimmers who paddle inside and people who come close for a photo can all push a seal back into the water. Disturbance of pupping caves is listed among the main threats to the species, alongside drowning in fishing nets, deliberate killing and the loss of quiet coastline.

Good news, with a warning

In June 2023 the IUCN moved the species from Endangered to Vulnerable, after years of protection work. That is real progress, but the population is still tiny. In Türkiye, researchers estimate that only about 120 adults remain, and they say coastal construction keeps squeezing the places seals can use.

If you meet a monk seal

  • Keep your distance and stay quiet. Watch from far away, and never try to touch, feed or swim with a seal.
  • Never enter a seal cave, by boat or by swimming. In Türkiye, conservationists point out that entering monk seal caves is against the rules.
  • Turn tours away. Don't book trips that promise to “show you a seal” in its cave.
  • Report the sighting to a local conservation group, with the place and time. Photos taken from a distance help researchers.
