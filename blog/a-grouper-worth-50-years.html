Gus the grouper: a fish worth 50 years

18 November 2026 · 4 min read

“I was a girl for twelve years,” says Gus. “Then I grew up.” It sounds like a joke. For a dusky grouper, it's simply how life works.

Gus is a dusky grouper (Epinephelus marginatus), the best-known grouper of the Mediterranean. Groupers are big, heavy-headed fish with wide mouths. They live among rocks, usually in water shallower than 50 metres, and hunt fish, crabs and octopuses. The largest can reach 1.5 metres in length.

Female first, male later

Dusky groupers are born female. They start breeding at around five years old. Somewhere between their 9th and 16th year, most often around 12, some of them turn into males. In European waters this change usually happens when the fish is 80 to 90 centimetres long.

In the Mediterranean they spawn between June and September. A big male holds a territory and guards it fiercely, and each male breeds with a group of females, around seven of them on average.

So a population needs its big, old fish. Without them there are no males, and with no males there is no next generation.

A slow life meets fast fishing

A grouper can live for about 50 years. That slow life is the problem. A fish that takes more than a decade to become a male can't replace itself as fast as people catch it. Spearfishers and fishers have always gone for the biggest groupers, which are exactly the ones a population can least afford to lose.

The numbers show what happened. In seven countries that make up a large part of the species' range, grouper catches fell by 88 per cent between 1990 and 2001. Today the IUCN lists the dusky grouper as Vulnerable.

Where groupers are coming back

Groupers stay close to home, so they respond well when an area is properly protected.

  • Medes Islands, Spain. After more than 20 years of protection, scientists found that the grouper population had almost reached the most the area can support.
  • Port-Cros, France. When protection began, there were very few groupers. After 50 years, divers were seeing young groupers and a higher share of females again, and the fish had spread across the park.
  • The French coast. Since France banned spearfishing for groupers in 1993, populations have slowly recovered. They are still mostly limited to protected areas.

That last point matters. Across the Mediterranean, fully protected areas cover only 0.04 per cent of the sea. Outside them, groupers are still in trouble.

One thing you can do

Leave grouper off your plate. If it's on a restaurant menu, choose something else and tell the waiter why. A 50-year-old fish can't be replaced in one summer.
