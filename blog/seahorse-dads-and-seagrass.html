Seahorse dads and the seagrass they need

17 November 2026 · 4 min read

“In my family, dads give birth,” says Pip. It sounds like a joke. It's one of the strangest true facts in the sea.

Pip is a seahorse. In the Mediterranean, the most common species is the long-snouted seahorse (Hippocampus guttulatus), about 15 centimetres long. Seahorses are fish, but they swim slowly and spend much of their time holding on to seagrass with their curling tails.

How a seahorse dad gives birth

The female places her eggs in a pouch on the male's belly. He fertilises them and carries them while they develop. Depending on the species, a male can carry anywhere from 50 to 1,500 eggs. Inside the pouch he controls the water's salt levels and makes compounds that protect the eggs from infection; around 3,000 genes play a part in this male pregnancy.

When the young are ready, the father has contractions and pushes them out into the water. From that moment they are on their own, and only about 1 per cent are likely to grow up.

A nursery made of seagrass

Many of the Mediterranean's seahorses live in meadows of Posidonia oceanica, a seagrass found only in this sea. The meadows give seahorses something to hold on to, food to hunt and places to hide. They also protect beaches from erosion and store carbon.

Posidonia grows very slowly, so damage lasts a long time. One of its biggest threats on busy coasts is something most boaters never think about: the anchor.

What an anchor does

Field experiments in Italy showed that the most damage happens when the anchor is pulled up and drags through the meadow. A study in Gökova Bay noted that a single anchoring can damage up to six Posidonia shoots. That sounds small until you multiply it by every boat, every day, all summer. In one heavily used bay in Corsica, researchers mapped a loss of 72.9 hectares of seagrass linked to recreational anchoring.

How boaters can help

  • Anchor on sand, never on seagrass. From the boat, sand looks pale and seagrass looks dark green or brown.
  • Use mooring buoys where they exist.
  • Lift the anchor straight up instead of motoring forward and dragging it.
